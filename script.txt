Video 1: The Institutional Investor Ban — What It Means for You (Title X)

Hey everyone, Mike Savory here with Real Property Management Excel. The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act just became law, and it's the biggest piece of federal housing legislation in years, at 139 pages and twelve titles. Over the next few videos, I'm going to break down what each major part means for you as a small residential real estate investor. And we're starting with the headline: the institutional investor ban.

Here's what the law actually says. Investors who control 350 or more single-family homes can no longer purchase additional ones. That took effect about six months after the bill was signed, and it stays in place for 15 years. The penalties have real teeth — up to a million dollars per violation, or three times the purchase price, whichever is greater.

Now, the first thing you need to know: if you own fewer than 350 homes — which you likely do — this ban does not apply to you. You can keep buying. In fact, your competitive position just got better, because the biggest cash buyers in the entry-level market have been sidelined.

But before you celebrate, let's talk about the fine print, because there are a lot of exceptions. Big investors can still buy new construction, build-to-rent communities, homes needing substantial renovation — at least fifteen percent of the purchase price in improvements — 55-plus communities, and foreclosure-related acquisitions. So institutional capital isn't leaving residential real estate. It's being redirected — mostly toward building new rentals instead of buying existing homes.

What does this mean for your business? Three things.

First, acquisitions. In markets where hedge funds were outbidding everyone on entry-level homes, you'll see less of that. More inventory may actually reach the market, because nobody's forced to sell, but the biggest buyers are out of the resale game.

Second, your exit strategy. This cuts both ways. If your plan was ever to sell a rental portfolio to a large fund, that buyer pool just shrank. Portfolio sales to institutions now only work through narrow exceptions. Factor that into your long-term planning.

Third, rents. Here's the unintended consequence that doesn’t get talked about: removing institutional demand doesn't create a single new house. In markets where big investors supplied a lot of the rental inventory, rental supply could tighten — which may actually push rents up. Good for cash flow, but tough for tenants.

One more thing: the law creates a federal renter outreach hotline and website for tenants of large institutional owners, and those owners now have annual reporting requirements. That doesn't touch small landlords, but it signals where federal attention is headed — professional, responsive management is only becoming more important.

Bottom line: this law was written to move single-family homes from Wall Street to Main Street. As a small investor, you're on the right side of that line. In the next video, we'll look at something that could change how your lower-priced properties get bought and sold: small-dollar mortgages. See you there.

Video 2: Small-Dollar Mortgages — Unlocking the Sub-$100K Market (Titles I & IV)

Welcome back to this series of explainer videos on the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act. Today we're talking about a quiet provision in the Act that could have a big impact on anyone who owns lower-priced rental properties: small-dollar mortgages.

Here's the problem this solves. If you've ever tried to sell a house priced under $100,000, you know the buyer pool is thin — not necessarily because people don't want the house, but because they can't finance it. Lenders make roughly the same fixed costs on a $80,000 loan as a $400,000 loan, but a fraction of the revenue. So they simply don't write small mortgages. That's pushed the entire sub-$100K market into cash-only territory — which usually means selling to other investors at a discount.

The new law goes after this from two directions. First, FHA is authorized to launch a small-dollar mortgage pilot — that's loans of $100,000 or less on one-to-four-unit primary residences. The pilot can pay lenders incentives to originate these loans, and give buyers direct grants for down payments, closing costs, appraisals, and title insurance. Second, federal regulators are required to study the fee caps and compensation rules that made small loans unprofitable in the first place — that's the groundwork for permanent fixes.

So what does this mean for you as an investor?

Number one: exit liquidity. If you hold C-class or lower-priced rentals, your future buyer might be an owner-occupant with a mortgage instead of another investor with cash. Financed owner-occupants typically pay more than cash investors. That's a direct boost to the value of your lower-priced holdings.

Number two: tenant-to-buyer sales. Some of your current tenants may become qualified buyers for the very homes they're renting. A direct sale to your tenant — no vacancy, no make-ready, no commission in some cases — can be one of the cleanest exits there is.

Number three — and here's the caution: note that these loans are for primary residences only. This doesn't help you finance rental acquisitions. And it's a pilot program with a four-year sunset that HUD may establish — it isn't guaranteed, and the scale is unknown. Watch how it rolls out before you count on it.

The takeaway: for the first time in a long time, the federal government is trying to make cheap homes financeable. If your portfolio lives in that price range, this is worth tracking closely. Next video: free money for fixing up your rentals — seriously. See you then.

Video 3: Repair Money, Conversions, and the Supply Push (Title II)

Alright, this might be my favorite part of the bill for small investors: Title II, "Building More in America." Most of it is about boosting housing supply, but tucked inside is a program that could directly fund repairs on your rental properties.

It's called the Whole-Home Repairs program. Here's how it works: local implementing organizations will offer forgivable loans to small landlords for rehabilitating rental properties — individual units, common areas, structural elements. And "small landlord" is defined generously for our world: fewer than 10 rental properties, no more than 25 total units, majority-owned by you or your family.

The catch — and there's always a catch: the units have to be affordable, meaning rents capped relative to area median income, and you have to stay in compliance for up to three years after the repairs, at which point the loan can be fully forgiven. The loan is secured by a lien on the property in the meantime.

Should you do it? Run the numbers for your specific market. In a lot of workforce-housing submarkets, the affordability cap is close to what you're charging anyway. If that's you, this is essentially subsidized rehab in exchange for doing what you were already doing. If you're pushing top-of-market rents, the restriction will sting, and it may not pencil.

Quick hits on the rest of Title II, because these shape where opportunity shows up over the next decade:

HUD grants now give priority to projects in opportunity zones — if you invest in one, more federal money is heading toward your neighborhood.

The RESIDE Act funds converting empty commercial buildings — offices, retail — into housing. Watch your local market for conversion projects; they change neighborhood dynamics fast.

Cities can get grants for pre-approved "pattern book" home designs — meaning faster, cheaper permitting for small multifamily: duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes, townhomes, and ADUs. If you build or do heavy rehab, ask your city whether they're pursuing this.

And FHA's multifamily loan limits were roughly quadrupled and indexed to construction costs going forward. That mostly matters for larger apartment deals, but it means FHA-financed construction is viable again in high-cost areas — expect more multifamily supply in the pipeline.

The big picture: Washington is betting hard on supply. More supply eventually means more competition for your rentals — but the investors who get hurt by new supply are the ones who ignored it. The ones who benefit are the ones who use programs like whole-home repairs to keep their properties competitive. Next up: the manufactured housing revolution, and the easiest new financing for ADUs. See you there.

Video 4: Manufactured Homes and ADUs — New Tools for Adding Units (Title III)

Today, let's talk about the part of the ROAD to Housing Act that changes what you can physically put on a piece of land — and how you pay for it.

Start with what sounds like a technicality but isn't: the law eliminates the requirement that manufactured homes be built on a permanent steel chassis. That rule dates to the 1970s, and it's the reason manufactured homes have always looked and appraised like, well, trailers. Without it, factories can build homes that sit on regular foundations, look like site-built houses, and — this is the key part — may appraise and finance more like site-built houses. Factory construction typically costs significantly less per square foot than site-built. For investors, that could mean a legitimate low-cost path to adding rental inventory, especially on infill lots.

Second: modular housing. HUD is required to review and fix FHA construction financing rules that have shut modular builders out — including the draw schedules that don't match how factories get paid. Expect modular construction to get easier to finance over the next couple of years.

Third — and this is the one most of you can use soonest: FHA Title I home improvement loans now explicitly cover building accessory dwelling units. ADUs. The loan limits were raised substantially and will now adjust annually with an index, and repayment terms can run up to 30 years.

Think about what that means. If you own a rental — or a personal residence — with a decent backyard in a jurisdiction that allows ADUs, there's now a federally insured loan product designed to fund building a second unit on that lot. An ADU is one of the most capital-efficient ways to add a rental door: you already own the land, and in most markets a detached one-bedroom rents remarkably well.

Two cautions. First, local zoning still rules — federal law doesn't force your county to allow ADUs, so check local ordinances first. Second, the new manufactured home standards require HUD rulemaking, which takes time. The chassis-free homes aren't rolling off assembly lines yet.

Action items: find out your jurisdiction's ADU rules, and keep an eye on the new generation of manufactured homes as HUD finalizes standards. The cost-per-door math on both could be compelling. Video 5: Appraisal Appeals and Section 8 Made Easier (Titles IV & VII)

Today's video is about two operational changes in the ROAD to Housing Act that touch deals and decisions you're making right now: appraisals and Section 8.

First, appraisals. If you've ever had a purchase, sale, or refinance blow up because an appraisal came in low, listen up. The law now requires that lenders on all federally backed mortgages — FHA, VA, USDA, Fannie, Freddie — maintain a formal reconsideration of value process. That means when an appraisal comes in low, there's a required procedure for challenging it: submitting comps, flagging errors, getting a documented second look. It's not a guarantee of a higher number, but it turns "the appraisal is the appraisal" into a process you can actually work.

The law also attacks the appraiser shortage — creating a credentialed trainee pathway, letting certified appraisers use trainees on assignments, and funding workforce development. Fewer appraiser bottlenecks should eventually mean faster, cheaper appraisals. There's even a feasibility study for a public appraisal database, which could someday give you access to appraisal-level data when you're underwriting deals.

Practical tip: on your next refinance or sale with a low appraisal, ask the lender for their reconsideration of value procedure on day one. Come armed with your best comps. It's now their obligation to have that process.

Second: Section 8, officially the Housing Choice Voucher program. If you've avoided vouchers because of inspection hassles and vacancy time, the law made two landlord-friendly changes.

One: pre-approval inspections. If you're new to the program, you can now ask the housing authority to inspect your unit before a tenant ever applies. If it passes, that inspection counts for 60 days — and the housing authority gives voucher holders a list of pre-approved units. That flips the old sequence, where you found a tenant, then waited weeks for an inspection while your unit sat vacant.

Two: fewer duplicate inspections. Units already inspected under other programs — like tax-credit properties — can have that inspection count, and remote video inspections are now allowed in rural areas.

With rents softening in some submarkets, guaranteed government-backed rent deserves a fresh look, and these changes remove a real chunk of the friction that kept small landlords away.

Bottom line: appeal your low appraisals, and reconsider vouchers if you wrote them off years ago. One video left — a rapid-fire tour of everything else in the bill worth knowing, from rural loans to the ban on a digital dollar. See you there.

Video 6: The Rest of the Bill in Four Minutes (Titles V–IX, XI)

Last video in the series. We've covered the investor ban, small-dollar mortgages, repair money, manufactured housing, and the appraisal and Section 8 changes. Today: a rapid-fire tour of everything else in the ROAD to Housing Act that's worth a small investor's attention.

Title 5 is program reform. The headline for us: the Rural Housing Service — USDA's housing arm — gets a major modernization, streamlining its loan programs. If you invest in small towns or rural counties, USDA financing for your buyers, and rural rental programs, should get easier to work with. Title V also overhauls federal disaster recovery housing money, aiming to get rebuilding funds moving faster after storms — which matters to anyone holding property in a disaster-prone area, because how fast a community rebuilds determines whether your investment there recovers.

Title 6 covers veterans. Lenders and counselors will now ask a standard military service question so veterans get routed to VA loan benefits they've earned, and VA loan disclosures get clearer. If you're selling a property, remember: VA buyers can finance one hundred percent. Don't let listing agents steer you away from them.

Title 9 is about community banks — making it easier for them to gather deposits, form new banks, and keep lending locally. Here's why you care: community banks are the backbone of small-investor lending. They're who does your portfolio loans, your construction loans, your commercial refis. A healthier community banking sector means more competition for your business, and that's good for your rates and terms.

Titles 7 and 8 are oversight — annual testimony from housing regulators, interagency coordination, and a stack of GAO studies, including several on how all of this affects housing markets. Those studies are what future legislation gets built on, so the data collected over the next few years will shape round two.

And Title 11 — this one surprised people — bans the Federal Reserve from issuing a central bank digital currency without explicit approval from Congress, through 2030. Not directly a housing provision, but it ended up in this bill, and you'll hear about it in the news.

So let's zoom out on the whole law. Three big themes. One: single-family homes are being steered away from Wall Street and toward individuals — and small investors like you were deliberately left untouched. Two: there's a decade-long supply push coming — factory-built homes, ADUs, conversions, small multifamily. Three: the plumbing of our business — appraisals, inspections, small loans, rural programs — is getting real maintenance for the first time in years.

The investors who win in the next cycle will be the ones who understand these rules early and position accordingly.
