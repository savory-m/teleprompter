Video 3: Repair Money, Conversions, and the Supply Push

Alright, this might be my favorite part of the bill for small investors: Title II, "Building More in America." Most of it is about boosting housing supply, but tucked inside is a program that could directly fund repairs on your rental properties.

It's called the Whole-Home Repairs program. Here's how it works: local implementing organizations will offer forgivable loans to small landlords for rehabilitating rental properties — individual units, common areas, structural elements. And "small landlord" is defined generously for our world: fewer than 10 rental properties, no more than 25 total units, majority-owned by you or your family.

The catch — and there's always a catch: the units have to be affordable, meaning rents capped relative to area median income, and you have to stay in compliance for up to three years after the repairs, at which point the loan can be fully forgiven. The loan is secured by a lien on the property in the meantime.

Should you do it? Run the numbers for your specific market. In a lot of workforce-housing submarkets, the affordability cap is close to what you're charging anyway. If that's you, this is essentially subsidized rehab in exchange for doing what you were already doing. If you're pushing top-of-market rents, the restriction will sting, and it may not pencil.

Quick hits on the rest of Title II, because these shape where opportunity shows up over the next decade:

HUD grants now give priority to projects in opportunity zones — if you invest in one, more federal money is heading toward your neighborhood.

The RESIDE Act funds converting empty commercial buildings — offices, retail — into housing. Watch your local market for conversion projects; they change neighborhood dynamics fast.

Cities can get grants for pre-approved "pattern book" home designs — meaning faster, cheaper permitting for small multifamily: duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes, townhomes, and ADUs. If you build or do heavy rehab, ask your city whether they're pursuing this.

And FHA's multifamily loan limits were roughly quadrupled and indexed to construction costs going forward. That mostly matters for larger apartment deals, but it means FHA-financed construction is viable again in high-cost areas — expect more multifamily supply in the pipeline.

The big picture: Washington is betting hard on supply. More supply eventually means more competition for your rentals — but the investors who get hurt by new supply are the ones who ignored it. The ones who benefit are the ones who use programs like whole-home repairs to keep their properties competitive. Next up: the manufactured housing revolution, and the easiest new financing for ADUs.
