Script 1: Thinking About Renting Out Your House? Watch This First

Maybe you're moving for a new job. Maybe you've got a low interest rate you don't want to give up. And somebody told you, "Just rent it out."
It can be a great move. But it's also a business, and the first few decisions matter most. I'm Mike Savory with Real Property Management Excel, and here are six things to know before you hand over the keys.

1. Run the real numbers, not the easy ones.
Most people compare the rent to the mortgage payment and stop there. If rent is higher, they think they're making money.
But your real costs include property taxes, insurance, repairs, time sitting empty between tenants, and management, whether that's a company or your own time.

If the numbers only work when everything goes perfectly, they don't work.

2. Find out what it will actually rent for.
Not what you need it to rent for. Not what an online estimate says. What similar homes nearby have actually rented for recently.
Price it too high and it sits empty. Every empty month is a full month of rent you never get back.

3. Look at it through a tenant's eyes.
You've lived with that dripping faucet and the scuffed hallway for years. A tenant notices them on the first showing.
Fix the small stuff, deep clean, and make sure everything works. A rent-ready home rents faster, and usually to better tenants.

4. Know the Georgia rules.
Georgia law now requires rentals to be fit to live in and kept in repair. Security deposits are capped at two months' rent. And there are specific notice steps before you can file for eviction.

I made a whole video on these changes. I'll link it right here.

This is also the time to call your lender and your insurance agent. Renting out your home can change what your mortgage and your policy require, and you want to know before a tenant moves in.

5. Screen every applicant the same way.
Write down your requirements before you take a single application: income, rental history, credit, background. Then apply them the same way to everyone.
That's how you pick good tenants. It's also how you stay on the right side of fair housing law.

6. Decide who's going to manage it.
Plenty of people manage their own rental, and do it well. If you live nearby, have some flexibility, and don't mind a call about a broken water heater, self-managing can work.

If you're moving away, have a busy career, or just don't want the calls, a property manager handles the marketing, screening, rent collection, and maintenance for you.
Neither answer is wrong. Just make the choice on purpose.
