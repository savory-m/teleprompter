Video 5: Appraisal Appeals and Section 8 Made Easier (Titles IV & VII)

Today's video is about two operational changes in the ROAD to Housing Act that touch deals and decisions you're making right now: appraisals and Section 8.

First, appraisals. If you've ever had a purchase, sale, or refinance blow up because an appraisal came in low, listen up. The law now requires that lenders on all federally backed mortgages — FHA, VA, USDA, Fannie, Freddie — maintain a formal reconsideration of value process. That means when an appraisal comes in low, there's a required procedure for challenging it: submitting comps, flagging errors, getting a documented second look. It's not a guarantee of a higher number, but it turns "the appraisal is the appraisal" into a process you can actually work.

The law also attacks the appraiser shortage — creating a credentialed trainee pathway, letting certified appraisers use trainees on assignments, and funding workforce development. Fewer appraiser bottlenecks should eventually mean faster, cheaper appraisals. There's even a feasibility study for a public appraisal database, which could someday give you access to appraisal-level data when you're underwriting deals.

Practical tip: on your next refinance or sale with a low appraisal, ask the lender for their reconsideration of value procedure on day one. Come armed with your best comps. It's now their obligation to have that process.

Second: Section 8, officially the Housing Choice Voucher program. If you've avoided vouchers because of inspection hassles and vacancy time, the law made two landlord-friendly changes.

One: pre-approval inspections. If you're new to the program, you can now ask the housing authority to inspect your unit before a tenant ever applies. If it passes, that inspection counts for 60 days — and the housing authority gives voucher holders a list of pre-approved units. That flips the old sequence, where you found a tenant, then waited weeks for an inspection while your unit sat vacant.

Two: fewer duplicate inspections. Units already inspected under other programs — like tax-credit properties — can have that inspection count, and remote video inspections are now allowed in rural areas.

With rents softening in some submarkets, guaranteed government-backed rent deserves a fresh look, and these changes remove a real chunk of the friction that kept small landlords away.

Bottom line: appeal your low appraisals, and reconsider vouchers if you wrote them off years ago. One video left — a rapid-fire tour of everything else in the bill worth knowing, from rural loans to the ban on a digital dollar. See you there.
