Script 2: Georgia Landlord Law Changed. Here's What the Safe at Home Act Means for You

For years, Georgia had some of the most landlord-friendly rental laws in the country. When the Safe at Home Act passed in 2024, and a few important rules changed.

If you own a rental in Georgia, or you're thinking about buying one, here's what you need to know, in plain English.

I'm Mike Savory with Real Property Management Excel. Quick note before we start: I'm a property manager, not an attorney, so this is general information, not legal advice.

Change 1: Rentals must be fit to live in.
This is the big one. Every Georgia residential lease now includes a promise that the home is fit for human habitation. And the landlord has to keep it in repair during the lease.

This includes things like working heat and air, running water, electricity, a roof that doesn't leak. The basics that make a home livable.

Before this law, a judge had limited ability to order a landlord to make those repairs. Now that standard can be enforced in court.

Here's what this means for you: respond to repair requests quickly, keep records of what you fixed and when, and stay ahead of maintenance instead of waiting for things to break.

Change 2: Security deposits are capped at two months' rent.
For leases signed or renewed on or after July 1, 2024, you can't collect a security deposit of more than two months' rent.

Most landlords were already under that. But if you've been charging more, especially for applicants with weaker credit, you'll need another approach. One option is a security deposit alternative, which lowers the tenant's move-in cost while still protecting you.

Change 3: More notice before an eviction filing.
If a tenant falls behind on rent, you now have to give them written notice and wait at least three business days before you can file for eviction.

It sounds small. But skipping that step can get your case thrown out, and that costs you weeks of time and lost rent.

Here's a quick checklist of things you should do now:

First, review your lease. If you downloaded a generic template from the internet, it may not reflect Georgia law today.
Second, check your deposit amounts, especially on renewals.
Third, document the property's condition at move-in, with photos and dates - and have your tenant sign off on it.
And fourth, put a written process in place for late rent, so the right notice goes out every time.

The Safe at Home Act doesn't make owning a rental in Georgia a bad idea. It just raises the bar on doing it right.
